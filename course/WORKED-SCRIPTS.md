# Worked Scripts — Original Models, Read for Decisions

## Keep the material types separate

- **REAL VIDEO:** a published video by another creator. The source Atlas analyzes it through paraphrased captions and approximate timecodes. We observe and infer carefully; we do not claim to know private intent.
- **ORIGINAL SCRIPT:** a new learning script written for the Atlas. It is not a published creator’s transcript and is not automatically a true personal story. Any experiment must be performed before it is presented in the past tense.
- **WORKSHOP:** an activity for the learner. It asks you to create a brief, outline, rewrite, or production decision; it is not passive reading.

The source originals remain in the supplied archive: `05-long-scripts-ab.html`, `06-long-script-c.html`, and `07-short-scripts.html`. This page teaches how to study their decisions without forcing every full script into the core reading sequence.

## Model A — *I Tried Building One Cinematic AI Scene Without Faking the Story*

**Type:** Original script · process documentary / educational hybrid · planned length approximately 8–10 minutes.  
**Core thesis:** visual consistency serves a readable choice; it does not create story by itself.  
**Publication condition:** the images, tests, viewer responses, and result must be real, or the script must identify them as a demonstration or hypothetical.

### Walk-through: why each section exists

| Section | What it does | Why it works | What would break it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold open: consistent face, disconnected decision** | Shows a visible contradiction and promises one small scene test | The viewer can inspect the problem immediately; the scope stays small | “I made a film in ten minutes” would overclaim; footage not yet created cannot be reported as fact |
| **Rule: one character, place, three shots, one choice** | Defines the experiment and a success condition | Limits prevent endless tool exploration; the audience knows what counts as success | Saying “the method solves AI filmmaking” would exceed the test |
| **First attempt: attractive objects, no causal sequence** | Gives the audience a concrete failure to diagnose | The problem is not called “bad”; it is shown in the relation between shots | Generic claims about the result without playing the sequence would remove the proof |
| **Reference sheet: visible and mental continuity** | Shows a technical fix, then includes what the character knows | The example progresses from appearance to intention; the second idea deepens the first | Treating a prompt as a guaranteed solution would turn a model into an unsupported rule |
| **Turn: images match; the story still does not** | Changes the central question from consistency to choice | A fix reveals a more important obstacle; the plan genuinely changes | If the second problem were invented just to create a reversal, the story would be false |
| **Shot order and sound: wide / medium / detail** | Assigns each shot a different meaning and lets audio support the decision | The camera scale is not decorative; each shot carries new information | Repeating the same visual three times or adding dramatic music as proof would weaken it |
| **Prototype and objection** | Lets the viewer watch without a voice telling them what to feel; names what remains wrong | The result is honest and the audience can judge it | A fake flaw or fake success would borrow trust |
| **Small comprehension check** | Asks what a few viewers understood, with consent and limits | The writer tests the meaning, not popularity | A few responses cannot establish audience-wide performance or causal impact |
| **Payoff: four questions for the next scene** | Returns to the opening problem with a transferable tool | The answer is useful beyond the one demonstration | A sales CTA before the method would make the payoff feel withheld |

### A short excerpt, annotated

> “The face is almost identical in all three shots. Same jacket. Same hair. Same city. And yet, when I watch them together, I don’t believe this is the same person making a single decision.”

The first sentences give the viewer things to see; the “and yet” changes the question. The line moves from **surface continuity** to **story continuity**. It is an honest hook only if the three shots really support that description. If they do not, the writer must change the evidence and the promise.

> “One character, one location, three shots, one choice.”

This is a constraint. It makes the experiment easier to judge. Constraints can help a video because they reduce unrelated variables, but they do not automatically create scientific control. The script correctly describes a bounded scene test, not a universal result.

> “The problem wasn’t that the images were ugly. The problem was that nothing in shot two caused shot three.”

This line translates a vague judgment into a causal diagnosis. It also previews the next repair. If the images were in fact unattractive or the issue was different, the line should be rewritten to match the evidence.

> “Continuity isn’t only a face. It’s also a mind that remembers what just happened.”

The phrase “a mind that remembers” is a metaphor for information continuity. The shots must preserve not only costume but what the character knows and why they act. The script can then show the letter entering the sequence as new information.

### Production and ethical notes

- The first and revised footage must be actual versions from the project or labeled demonstrations.
- Any tool, version, date, attempt count, viewer response, cost, and licensing statement is **[VERIFY]**.
- A small viewer check can test whether the action is understandable to those viewers. It cannot predict platform performance.
- If the result fails, keep the result honest. The ending can be: “The scene still does not read clearly; here is the next thing I would test.”

## Model B — *Why Your First 30 Seconds Feel Slow (And How to Fix Them)*

**Type:** Original script · educational explainer with a visible before/after.  
**Core distinction:** “slow” means delayed clarity in this lesson—not necessarily a quiet image or slow speaking rate.

### Section walkthrough

1. **Two openings side by side:** the viewer sees a vague version and a clearer version. This sets up a comparison instead of merely announcing advice.
2. **Define slow:** explain that a quiet opening can work if the question is clear. This prevents a false rule such as “cut faster.”
3. **Three questions:** who is this for, what is being answered, and what evidence can support the answer? The test provides a reusable tool.
4. **Rewrite pass:** remove or move greeting and generic context because they arrive before a reason to care. A line is moved only if it has a better later use; not every introduction is forbidden.
5. **Bring proof forward:** play the actual old and new drafts. The file, sentence, and timing must be real—or labeled as a demo.
6. **Three valid openings:** direct promise, scene, and question. The script explains when each is available and warns against a made-up personal anecdote.
7. **Exception:** quiet openings may need room; the test is deliberate meaning, not a stopwatch.
8. **Viewer exercise and payoff:** the audience identifies the first concrete image, real question, and first proof, then hears the revision at normal pace.

The key teaching move is **definition → demonstration → editing decision → exception → learner practice**. It does not say that every video should start with a before/after, or that a fast opening guarantees views.

## Model C — *I Made 12 Shorts from One Long Video. Here’s What I Learned.*

**Type:** Original script · experiment plan with explicit conditions and limits.  
**Core question:** what changes when each Short is written to stand alone instead of being cut from the long video?

The script compares three approaches: direct clips, clips with rewritten openings/endings, and independently written mini-stories. It asks for twelve real Shorts, production records, and actual channel data. It warns that timing, audience, topic, and footage are not identical, so the comparison is not a controlled scientific study. The result section contains **[VERIFY]** fields; if the experiment has not happened, the script must be a future plan, not a false report.

The transferable lesson is that the long video is a source of ideas, not a bin of clips that must all be used. Some moments already stand alone; others need context or a new ending; some need a new video. The Short still needs an independent question and payoff.

## Six Original Short Scripts — What Each One Teaches

The source’s six full scripts are in `07-short-scripts.html`. Use them as model scripts after you have practiced a short of your own.

| Short | Format / skill | Strong decision | Common misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **“The missing decision”** | Educational | Turns endless research into a question that can guide the next source | Telling viewers to research less when verification is necessary |
| **“One character, three shots”** | Visual before/after | Shows a missing change between shots; the image carries proof | Explaining every prompt in a Short whose promise is only the sequence problem |
| **“Your thumbnail is a question”** | Design / hook | Aligns a visible question with the actual video answer | Claiming one color guarantees views |
| **“The quiet opening”** | Counterexample | Uses silence because a visible deletion has a meaningful cost | Adding silence when the image contains no question |
| **“Not a robot, not a lie”** | Spoken script / generated voice | Fixes the thought before tuning the voice | Using voice tools to conceal identity or make an unverified result sound proven |
| **“The sales line that doesn’t ruin the video”** | CTA / business | Gives the full method before offering a useful worksheet | Hiding a step to force a purchase or click |

### One short, walked through: “One character, three shots”

- **Opening question:** “Same character. Three shots. Why does this still feel like three separate stories?” The visual is the question; it must be the learner’s footage or a labeled demo.
- **First answer:** the face is consistent but intention is not. This is a partial payoff, not an answer withheld until the end.
- **Change:** add one piece of information so the second shot changes what the character knows.
- **Final proof:** replay the three-shot sequence with little or no narration. The viewer can judge whether the choice now reads.
- **Takeaway:** continuity includes what the character remembers and chooses, not only the same coat.

If the new sequence does not read, change the script’s conclusion. The model is not permission to claim a successful result in advance.

## Compare a Script with a Real-Video Analysis

A real-video analysis answers: **what can I observe in an existing video, and what might that choice be doing?** An original script answers: **what could an honest, producible script look like for a new project?** A workshop asks: **what will I make or test now?** Keep those questions separate. The real-video creator’s words and life are not interchangeable with a model script’s fictional placeholders or with your own experience.

## Model-Script Assignment

Choose one original script in the ZIP. Before reading its annotations:

1. Write the promise, first proof, first turn, and ending.
2. Mark one line you would cut and one line you would protect.
3. Identify a personal claim, number, or result that requires verification.
4. Explain what the visual or sound direction contributes.
5. Build a five-beat outline for a different topic using the same *function* but new evidence and wording.

A strong analysis explains why the choice works, when it would not work, and what the replacement needs. It does not merely praise the final draft.
