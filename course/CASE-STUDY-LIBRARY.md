# Case-Study Library — Study the Decisions, Not the Lines

The source Atlas contains a 13-video analysis library and three deeper walkthroughs. The library is optional: do not turn thirteen separate analyses into thirteen required lessons. First learn a tool in the core course, then use one relevant case to test whether you can notice the tool in a real video.

## Before You Watch

The source analyses describe publicly available videos through paraphrased captions and approximate timecodes. They are not full transcripts. Captions can be wrong, timestamps can drift, and text alone cannot show every visual, performance, music, or edit decision. The analyses cannot prove a creator’s private intentions or causal effects on audience metrics. Open the original video, inspect the relevant sequence, and mark your notes as:

- **Observed:** directly visible or audible in the video.
- **Inference:** a plausible explanation of the observed choice.
- **Preference:** an alternative you would test, not a fact about what the creator should have done.

Do not copy a creator’s wording, joke, personal history, result, or performance persona. Transfer the *decision*—the function of the opening, example, turn, or payoff—to new material of your own.

## The Five-Step Analysis Method

1. **Watch once for the contract.** What viewer and question does the opening appear to promise? What do title and thumbnail lead you to expect?
2. **Watch again for movement.** At three or four time points, write what the viewer knows before and after. Mark first proof, change, counterpoint, and payoff.
3. **Inspect channels.** What is said, shown, and heard? Does the image add evidence or duplicate VO? What does music or silence do?
4. **Ask why / why not.** What is useful about the choice? Under what audience, footage, or evidence would another choice be better?
5. **Transfer.** Write an original outline for a different topic using the function—not the original words, examples, or personal claims.

Use the reusable [case-study card](WORKBOOK.md#reusable-case-study-card). Pause before reading the analysis answer in any source page. The point is to make your own prediction first.

## Three Recommended Deep Dives

These correspond to the source Atlas’s full-video deep dives. The times below are approximate navigation aids, not frame-accurate transcripts.

### A. Kallaway — “How To Become A Master Storyteller”

**Watch for:** a structured educational progression; an example that makes an abstract point usable; the shift from story connection to sentence rhythm; viewer-focused framing; and a hook/image relationship.

| Approximate point | What to inspect | Transfer question |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00–00:30 | Topic and learning contract | Does a beginner know what skill the video will teach? |
| 00:30–02:30 | Context/conflict and “and then” versus “but / therefore” | Does the relationship between events actually change—or is a connector being pasted on? |
| 02:30–03:30 | A concrete example (the source analysis mentions a Stanley example) | Does the example demonstrate the rule, or only decorate it? |
| 03:30–05:30 | Sentence length and rhythm | Can you hear the craft being demonstrated? Are short and long sentences varied? |
| 05:30–08:20 | Speaking to a viewer and thinking about an ending | How does the explanation move from principle to audience use? |
| 08:20–10:50 | Lens and promise/image alignment | What expectation does the opening create, and what visual evidence supports it? |
| 10:50–end | A relevant next step | Does the CTA follow delivered value? |

**Why this case is useful:** It connects story logic, spoken rhythm, audience address, and structure in one tutorial. Do not impose conflict on a sequence that has no real causal relation; do not borrow the creator’s examples or personal style.

### B. Isaac — “How I Actually Make AI Voice Sound Real”

**Watch for:** a concrete opening problem, a principle before the tool tour, audible comparisons, an imperfect result, and the relationship among writing, performance, sound generation, and editing.

| Approximate point | What to inspect | Transfer question |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00–01:30 | Demonstration of an unnatural exchange, then a guiding principle | Could an A/B sound example teach this faster than an abstract claim? |
| 01:30–05:30 | Diagnosis of voice, pauses, and the script before tool selection | Which problem belongs to wording and which belongs to delivery or software? |
| 06:00–09:30 | Tool choices, settings, failures, and comparison | Can the viewer hear what changed? Are settings presented as version-specific rather than universal? |
| 10:00–13:20 | Combining generations and considering authenticity | What extra editing work is hidden by a “one-click” description? |
| 14:00–17:20 | An additional use case (dubbing) | Does the extra module deepen the promise or start a second video? |
| 17:30–end | Outcome and analytics | Is the result reported honestly, even if it is less impressive than expected? |

**Why this case is useful:** It shows why a good tool cannot repair a script no human would say. It also connects narration with performance and post-production. Do not copy a voice, hide a generated voice where disclosure is appropriate, or state that a setting works across versions without checking.

### C. Ali Abdaal — “How to Invest for Beginners”

**Watch for:** a beginner’s question before specialist terminology, a sequence from why to what/how/trade-offs, examples, and limits. This is a **structure study, not investment advice**. Numbers, products, financial rules, and examples can be dated or jurisdiction-specific.

| Approximate point | What to inspect | Transfer question |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00–00:50 | The beginner’s uncertainty | Does the opening start from a real viewer problem instead of a dictionary? |
| 00:50–02:40 | Why the question matters before more terminology | What foundation does this audience need first? |
| 03:00–07:30 | Concrete examples of assets and ownership | Does the example make an abstract idea visible without overclaiming? |
| 09:00–15:30 | Risk and a possible alternative (such as a fund) | Does the risk appear before the option is presented as a solution? |
| 17:00–23:30 | Historical movement, time, and limits | Are historical examples kept separate from predictions? |
| 25:00–end | Personal context and further resources | Is personal experience clearly limited, and does the ending recognize that one video cannot teach everything? |

**Why this case is useful:** It illustrates how an educational video can introduce concepts in the order a beginner needs, rather than the order an expert learned them. Do not carry its financial claims into a new script without current, independent verification.

## Transfer Challenge

Choose a case and watch the opening, one middle turn, and the ending. Before you read the source analysis, write:

1. What does the opening make you expect?
2. What new information changes the next beat?
3. What does the video show that the words alone cannot prove?
4. What would you change for a different viewer—and what evidence would that require?
5. What function can you reuse with an entirely different example?

A good answer describes an observable decision and a condition for its use. It does not say, “I will write in this creator’s style.”
