# Reference Desk — Terms and Decision Cards

A term is useful only when it helps you choose, write, or diagnose. These are plain-English reminders; the lessons teach how to use them.

## Compact Glossary

| Term | Simple meaning | Example / why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **A-roll** | Main speaking or interview footage | The creator explains why a shot order changed. Useful when the speaker’s face, action, or presence carries meaning. |
| **Angle** | The particular question or viewpoint you choose inside a topic | Topic: video editing. Angle: why a polished scene can still have an unclear choice. It creates a boundary for the script. |
| **Audience contract** | What the viewer reasonably expects from the title, image, opening, and body | A thumbnail asks “Which cut reads?” The video must compare the cuts, not switch to an unrelated tool list. |
| **Beat** | One meaningful change in action, information, decision, or understanding | A clue appears; now the viewer understands why the character stopped. A beat is more than a heading. |
| **B-roll** | Supporting footage | A shot of the actual timeline comparison. It should clarify or prove, not just fill a gap. |
| **Cadence** | The audible pattern of stresses, lengths, and pauses | A long explanation followed by “The test failed.” Cadence helps emphasis and comprehension. |
| **Case study** | One documented situation examined for decisions and limits | Context → choice → outcome → what may transfer. A case does not establish a universal rule. |
| **Clarity** | The audience can recover the intended meaning without guessing | “I moved shot two before the letter appears” is clearer than “I optimized the sequence.” |
| **Cold open** | A first scene or demonstration before a longer explanation | Play the confusing cut, then explain why it matters. It must still match the promise. |
| **Conflict / resistance** | Something that complicates a desired result | The character’s face matches, but the reason for the next action is missing. Conflict need not be dangerous. |
| **Concision** | Removing what does not help, without deleting necessary nuance | Cut a repeated definition; keep a caveat that changes the claim. Shorter is not automatically clearer. |
| **Counterpoint** | A reasonable alternative or limit that could change the conclusion | Another change happened at the same time as the thumbnail test. It keeps the argument honest. |
| **CTA** | A call to action | “The full method is here; the blank checklist is below if you want to try it.” It should follow delivered value. |
| **Curiosity gap** | A relevant question the viewer wants answered | “Why did the final shot become harder to understand?” It is useful only if the video answers it. |
| **Exposition / context** | Information needed to understand what is happening | Explain where the footage came from before interpreting a comparison. Too much context too early can delay relevance. |
| **Foreshadowing** | A fair earlier clue that matters later | Show the letter before the choice depends on it. A random close-up is not a clue just because a script calls it one. |
| **Hook** | An opening contract that gives a particular viewer a reason to continue | A visible before/after and a clear promise. A hook is not a volume setting. |
| **Information architecture** | The order in which facts are taught | Problem → necessary definition → example → use. Order affects what a beginner can understand. |
| **Inference** | An explanation drawn from facts, but not directly observed | CTR rose after two changes; attributing the rise to one of them is an inference. Label it and consider alternatives. |
| **Micro-payoff** | A useful partial answer given before the final answer | Show that matching the coat fixed continuity but not story logic. It rewards attention while a deeper question remains. |
| **Narrative** | A purposeful chain of changes, not simply a list of events | A choice changes what happens next. A chronology can still be a useful tutorial, but it is not automatically a story. |
| **Open loop** | A relevant question left unresolved for now | “Will a viewer understand this silent scene?” Close it with the test or result. Do not hold an ordinary answer hostage. |
| **Payoff** | An answer or result earned by earlier setup | The final shot shows the choice the opening asked us to watch for. |
| **Premise** | The central situation or claim the video develops | A creator tests whether one three-shot scene can show a decision. It is narrower than “AI is changing film.” |
| **Proof / evidence** | Material that supports a claim within a stated scope | A real before/after cut supports what changed in that edit; it does not prove a platform-wide result. |
| **Reversal / turn** | New information changes the expected direction or plan | The face is fixed, but the story still fails; the creator changes the test. A turn needs a real change. |
| **Reveal** | Information shown at a moment when the audience can understand its significance | Show the clue before explaining the final choice. Do not withhold essential safety context for surprise. |
| **Retention** | Whether a viewer continues; in writing, sustained attention earned through useful progress | Answer one question, then open a meaningful next question. Do not treat the viewer as a metric to trap. |
| **Setup** | Earlier information that makes later information intelligible | Establish why the letter matters before the final choice. |
| **Shot list** | A record of shots and the purpose each serves | Wide shot orients; medium shot reveals reaction; detail shows the hand hesitating. It is not a shopping list of pretty angles. |
| **SFX** | Sound effect | Paper movement can draw attention to a decision. Sound should not create proof that is absent. |
| **Stakes** | What could be gained or lost | A failed test costs a reshoot. Ordinary, real consequences are enough. |
| **Thesis** | The answer or position the video will support and qualify | “Consistency helps, but story clarity also needs a change in information or choice.” It should be revisable if evidence changes. |
| **Tone** | The current emotional or formality setting | Careful during a financial explanation, playful during a harmless editing mistake. It can shift while the writer’s voice remains recognizable. |
| **Transition** | A bridge that explains why one beat follows another | “The face matched, but the action did not.” It shows a complication rather than only naming the next topic. |
| **VO / voiceover** | Narration heard over images or other footage | VO should explain meaning or cause, not automatically describe every visible click. It still needs to sound natural aloud. |
| **Voice** | The writer’s recognizable choices of observation, judgment, rhythm, and evidence | A repeated habit of naming what a test does not prove can be part of voice. It is not the same as slang. |
| **Visual hook** | An image that carries a relevant question or evidence | Three shots with a readable mismatch. A random spectacle is not a visual hook. |

## Desk Card: The Script

- Who is this for, and what do they know already?
- What question does the video answer?
- What is the promise—and what is outside its scope?
- What evidence can I show? What cannot it prove?
- What changes in each beat?
- What can be cut or moved without harming the promise?
- Does the ending pay the opening?
- What must be verified, filmed, licensed, or disclosed?

## Desk Card: The Hook

**Specific viewer → honest problem → available proof → first value → earned open loop.**  
A quiet opening can work when its image carries a question. A bold claim needs early evidence. The title and thumbnail are part of the promise.

## Desk Card: Research

**Claim → source → date → exact support → alternative → limit → script wording.**  
Popularity is not verification. A screenshot can show a number, not necessarily what caused it.

## Desk Card: Human Writing

**Observed action → owned decision → precise scope → natural mouth → no fake biography.**  
Remove inflated adjectives before hunting for “human” slang. Ask: who knows this, and how?

## Desk Card: Story

**Want → resistance → attempt → new information → choice → consequence → change.**  
If no real choice or consequence exists, choose an educational or list structure instead of inventing one.

## Desk Card: Long-Form

**One central promise → useful early proof → modules that add knowledge → real turn if earned → counterpoint → payoff → optional next step.**  
Do not write by runtime alone.

## Desk Card: Short-Form

**One independent question → legible context → proof → complete thought.**  
A viewer should not need the long video to understand the answer.

## Desk Card: Spoken + Visual

Read twice aloud. Mark breath. Test names. Watch muted. Listen audio-only. Hold proof long enough to inspect. Check captions on a phone. Let each channel carry the information it handles best.

## Desk Card: Revision

**Idea/promise → structure → clarity → value → proof → voice → spoken flow → repetition/pacing → visuals/production → final polish.**  
If a fact changes, return to the idea or outline. A polished sentence in the wrong script is still in the wrong place.

## Desk Card: Final Polish

Names, numbers, quotes, dates, and current claims verified. Sources and rights recorded. Personal stories true or labeled as examples. On-screen text readable. CTA honest. Title and thumbnail match. Files versioned. No unresolved blocker disguised as a finished line.
