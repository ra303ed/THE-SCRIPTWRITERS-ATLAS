### Quick idea test

- Can I state the question in one sentence?
- Is the answer testable, demonstrable, or researchable?
- Is the viewer’s change specific?
- Do I have a plausible evidence path?
- Is this a video—or still only a topic?

**Model:** Topic = AI filmmaking. Viewer = creator with attractive but disconnected clips. Question = why does the character still feel as if they are in different scenes? Proof = compare two versions of one sequence. Promise = identify one missing story change and use a four-question scene check. This is an example, not a claim that a test occurred.

## 4. Research and Claim Ledger

Copy a row for every important claim.

| Claim / exact wording | Type (observation, test, external fact, inference, opinion) | Source + access date | Exact support | What it does not show / alternative | Planned script line | Visual citation / permission | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  | [VERIFY] / ready / cut |

### Source checks

- Who created the source, and when?
- Is the method or sample visible?
- Does it directly support the exact script line?
- Is there an independent source for a disputed factual claim?
- Could timing, incentives, or another factor explain the observation?
- Are the quotation, image, footage, music, and interface usable?

### Safe wording ladder

- **Observation:** “After I changed X, I noticed Y.”
- **Scoped result:** “In this test, under these conditions, Y happened.”
- **Inference:** “This may suggest X; another explanation is Y.”
- **Overclaim to avoid:** “X always causes Y for everyone.”

For finance, health, legal, safety, and other high-stakes material, verify current information, scope, region, and professional context before publication.

## 8. Ten-Pass Revision Checklist

Run one pass at a time; return to an earlier pass if the evidence changes.

1. **Idea and promise:** Is this the right question for this viewer? Does the title match the final answer?
2. **Structure:** Does each beat earn the next? Does the ending fulfill the opening?
3. **Clarity:** Can a new viewer follow the terms, pronouns, causes, and transitions?
4. **Value and retention:** Does each section change understanding? Does useful proof arrive in time?
5. **Proof and truth:** Are claims supported, scoped, dated, and honest about alternatives?
6. **Language and voice:** Are verbs concrete? Can I defend the details? Is anything borrowed or generic?
7. **Spoken flow:** Can I say it twice naturally? Where are the breath and pronunciation problems?
8. **Repetition, emotion, pacing:** What repeats without adding meaning? Are stakes real? Where should we slow down?
9. **Visuals and production:** What proves the line? Are assets, rights, captions, and sound clear?
10. **Final polish:** Are names, numbers, quotations, title, disclosure, filenames, and handoff complete?

| Production readiness / ethics |  |  |  |  |

Use the course levels:

- **Needs Work:** the viewer cannot yet follow or trust this part. Identify the missing decision or proof.
- **Developing:** the intention is visible, but an example, transition, limit, or test is weak.
- **Strong:** the part works for the stated viewer and has support; note the next refinement or boundary.
- **Professional:** the choice is precise, defensible, producible, and suited to this project; explain why it works.

Feedback sentence: **“This part is [level] because [specific evidence]. The viewer may [effect]. Try [action], then check by [test].”**

## 10. Final Production Checklist

### Contract and content

- [ ] Title, thumbnail, opening, body, and ending make the same honest promise.
- [ ] The viewer and their prior knowledge are clear.
- [ ] Every open loop is answered or intentionally removed.
- [ ] The ending pays the promise before any optional CTA.

### Truth and rights

- [ ] Every important number, name, quote, product detail, and date is verified.
- [ ] The script distinguishes observation, inference, opinion, and external fact.
- [ ] Claims fit their evidence; counterpoints and limits appear where needed.
- [ ] Personal history is true; hypothetical or staged material is labeled.
- [ ] Sources, footage, music, images, interviews, interface captures, and permissions are tracked.
- [ ] Sponsorship, affiliate links, generated voice/footage, and other disclosures are appropriate and clear.

### Performance and production

- [ ] Read aloud at real pace; record actual timing and pronunciation notes.
- [ ] The performer has a clean recording copy.
- [ ] The editor has shots, proof, sources, rights, timing, captions, and open tasks.
- [ ] Text is readable on a phone; essential information is available across sound-on and sound-off use.
- [ ] Music and effects do not ask viewers to believe more than the evidence shows.
- [ ] Every [VERIFY] and [SHOOT] item is complete or an explicit production blocker.

### Learning after publication

Write three columns: **Predicted / Observed / Cannot Infer**. Use feedback and analytics to choose another test; do not claim one metric proves which line caused a result.

## Optional 30-Day Practice Plan

Keep the project small enough to finish. If research or production needs more time, expand the schedule instead of inventing an outcome.

| Days | Practice | Evidence of progress |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Write one specific observation a day in your own spoken English | Concrete action, no inflated adjectives |
| 4–7 | Write four different hooks for one true idea | Each has a different contract and available proof |
| 8–12 | Analyze two real videos with the case-study card | Separate observation, inference, and preference |
| 13–18 | Build one brief, source ledger, and outline; capture proof shots | Every beat has a job and a visual/evidence plan |
| 19–23 | Draft, read aloud, and rewrite; keep v1/v2 and a decision log | Changes address diagnosed problems |
| 24–27 | Record or assemble a rough cut; make one long/short pair | Both work in their own format |
| 28–30 | Get outside feedback, verify facts/rights, revise, and postmortem | The work is clearer and the next test is named |
